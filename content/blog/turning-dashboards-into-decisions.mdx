Most dashboards fail quietly. They get built, demoed, admired for a week — and
then nobody opens them. The data is correct, the charts are pretty, and yet no
decision ever changes. That's not an analytics problem; it's a **design**
problem.

## Start from the decision, not the data

Before writing a single query, I ask one question: *what decision should this
dashboard change, and who makes it?* If we can't name the decision and the
person, we're building a wall poster.

A useful dashboard answers a question the viewer already has:

- Which customers are about to churn — **this week**?
- Is this campaign worth the spend, or should we cut it?
- Where is margin leaking, and by how much?

## Cut everything that isn't the answer

The fastest way to kill adoption is to show everything you *can* measure.
Restraint is the feature. A good rule: every element on the screen either
supports the decision or gets deleted.

> If a chart doesn't change what someone does, it's decoration.

## Make the number trustworthy

People act on numbers they trust. That means one definition of "revenue," one
source of truth, and transformations that anyone can audit. A quick SQL sketch
of the kind of model I build under the hood:

```sql
select
  date_trunc('week', order_date) as week,
  count(distinct customer_id)    as active_customers,
  sum(net_revenue)               as revenue
from fact_orders
where order_date >= current_date - interval '90 days'
group by 1
order by 1;
```

Clean, dated, reproducible — the opposite of a spreadsheet that only one person
understands. Inline values like `net_revenue` should mean exactly one thing
across every report.

## Close the loop

The last step is the one most teams skip: **watch how the dashboard gets used**,
then cut what's ignored and sharpen what's clicked. A BI asset is a product, not
a deliverable — it earns its place by changing decisions, over and over.

If your dashboards look impressive but nobody acts on them, that's fixable. It
usually takes less rebuilding than you'd think.
